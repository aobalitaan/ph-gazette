Symbol of the Triumph of the Race

When the First Lady greeted you and said that you are the true heroes in this center, I was the first to applaud. The truth is, I didn't believe you could finish these houses in just a few days. But you proved that you could, because the project was completed in just 21 days.

The truth is, colleagues, this project will only succeed if it receives the protection and support of the people of Bataan, especially the town leaders of Morong. This project will succeed if all of you here in Morong, Bataan and your leaders support and help this project. Because, it cannot be denied, this project is the beginning of a new spirit. So it needs to be watered and nurtured by everyone. I request and implore all of you, especially those from Morong and Bataan, to support this project.

The First Lady and I should be in another province because it is campaign season. Nine days from now, we will vote in the local elections. It has been a long time since we had a local election so everyone, even those who are sick and cannot walk, will probably go down to vote.

Today, I should have been in Pangasinan, or in Baguio. But when it was mentioned to me that I was also needed here with the First Lady, because it was necessary to show, to demonstrate to the public the sympathy of the Filipino people and their national leaders to our suffering brothers and sisters affected by the calamity in Vietnam, I did not hesitate to come. Because Vietnam is our neighbor. If you head in that direction, you will reach Vietnam; if you take a small boat towards it, the first country you will reach is Vietnam. Because Vietnam is indeed our neighbor, their suffering is our suffering too.

You know, Morong and Bagac was the Western terminal of the last line of defense of the United States Armed Forces in the Far East in the fighting in 1942. On April 9 when that line collapsed, the western terminal of the line was Morong. Many battles were fought here in Morong, many of our brothers and sisters perished here. That's why when I was flying, while flying over Morong again and passing by Mount Samat, Mount Natib, Mount Silanganan—Mariveles and Layac Mountains are to the South, Natib and Silanganan Mountains are to the North to our right — I remembered that descent, that low passage from Pilar going to Bagac to Morong. I remembered all our brothers and sisters who died because of the war. We all know the hardship that results from war. We know the misery, deprivation and the death because we lost about one million people in the last war and even now during the fighting in Mindanao. Because I know that many of us who were soldiers then also fought there in Mindanao. You know that 500,000 to one million people were displaced—lost their homes, lost their livelihoods and many of them got sick and died during the first battles there in Marawi in 1972 and in Cotabato from 1973 to 1976. Many of them have not yet been able to return.

To tell the truth, Filipinos come from a lineage of refugees because there are accounts in our history about Datu Puti and Marikudo. What was Datu Puti if not a refugee? He was a refugee who came from Borneo. And Marikudo, what is his story? The truth is, Marikudo and Datu Puti are the origin of all the Filipino race. They were driven out by a cruel government that they fought against. According to history, Datu Puti returned and fought in Borneo. And the race that dominated here and ruled Manila, Ilocos and Pangasinan came from the South. So all of you, all of us can truly be said to come from a lineage of refugees.

We are actually a nation composed of many refugees. The various races that have come here are either Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Bornean, Malaysian, Thai and many others. When Kublai Khan sent a large naval contingent to attack the islands in the South China Sea, many of the refugees in Malaysia moved towards the Philippines, Malaysia, Singapore, and other Asian countries.

And so to our foreign guests, we say, we know how it is to be a refugee, for our history is replete with many instances of our tribes receiving refugees and allowing the infusion of new blood to strengthen the entire race.

Today, once again, we see different races throughout the world committing the most common errors of man-solving our problems by war which causes the dislocation of many people.

Today, however, we come to open a center in an effort to recover what has been partially lost of man’s humanity and integrity. Today, you see the very same nations who fought against each other on this soil—Japan against the United States and against the Philippines—coming together to establish a refugee center, which shall reaffirm once again man’s humanity for his fellow human, and perhaps, give us fresh hope that no matter how delicate the fabric of the international community, that fabric will not be torn apart because the great majority of men do believe in the maintenance of harmonious international relations and peace.

Isn't it surprising, comrades, that different countries are here today that in the past, as the First Lady mentioned in her speech, fought here. Who were those who fought here? The Japanese were our enemy and that of America. Now those who fought have met, united to reclaim the true honor of a person, which is to first think of his duty to his fellow human being and to God Almighty.

On the evening of July 20, 1979, I received a call from the American ambassador indicating the need for some kind of initiative in view of the fact that there was a stalemate in the Geneva Conference that had been called by the Secretary General of the United Nations, on the request or initiative of Prime Minister Thatcher of England. The story here was that the entire conference would fall if there was no offer of some kind for a refugee processing center.

The truth of the matter is that I have been trying to convince many of our friends in the ASEAN to set up such processing centers long before the Geneva Conference. Indonesia and the Philippines had agreed to reserve certain islands for this purpose. And so I immediately took up the suggestion and called up Minister Carlos P. Romulo who was in Geneva sick with fever. He was sick, slightly feverish but I woke him up and I said, “Tell them at the Geneva conference that the Filipino people will provide a piece of land to house the poor people displaced from Vietnam.” We in the Philippines will provide that land and that land is not in Palawan, but here in Bataan because I know that the people here in Bataan are the most compassionate, humane and God-fearing.

And so we made this offer. We are very happy that it broke the stale mate in Geneva. We are very happy that we have been able to help our friends in arriving at a solution to this problem.

Why Bataan and not Palawan? Why was Bataan chosen? Because Palawan is very far. When the costs were considered, it was seen that half of the cost of building a processing center would go to transportation. And every time we move things, we would be hindered or we would need to use our ships. And our ships are few. So we were forced to locate it closer, and now it is here in our beloved province of Bataan.

I mentioned that I shouldn't have come here. But to demonstrate to all of you, to your citizens here in Morong and Bagac, and the neighboring towns here in all of Bataan, that this project is your project, a project of the Filipino people, so we need to help each other.

Likewise, the First Lady said that we will also benefit from this project. Why? The equipment, goods, and food that will be used here should be purchased from the neighboring towns, in Morong and in various places. Fifty thousand, my countrymen. We know that the money to be spent will go to you because the goods to be used and consumed here in this center will come from you.

Perhaps, we can say that we are all fortunate. We are all fortunate because in giving, in showing our sympathy, we are the ones who will benefit.

We should therefore extend all our support to this project, I say that this is a national commitment, not only a commitment of your President, but a commitment of the Filipinos. Therefore, it is necessary that we extend all our support and cooperation to this project.

Perhaps some of you are hesitant. Those who think that this is our way of forcing people to leave their farms. That is not true. Your farms will continue. You can continue farming your fields. They will not be taken away from you. I heard some of the people asking, “Will this settlement expand so that it will mean our losing our farms which are not covered by homestead.” The answer is “No.” Unless you voluntarily give it up and unless we need it. And I don’t think we will need it. On the contrary, it will be necessary for you to farm properly so that you can sell your produce to the processing center and in the process you will gain, you will earn money and you will have a market for your products.

We also feel that there are many ways in which this particular project will be of help to you because it will show you that the Vietnamese, these Vietnamese are hardworking people. So do not be mistaken when you see them. You will see that they work tirelessly. It is good that we have a benchmark, a measure of our ability, characteristics. A measure of our wisdom and intelligence because we cannot say that those people are foolish. No! They are intelligent and hardworking.

I am Ilokano but they are perhaps more hardworking than the Ilokano and more frugal, right? I know that here in Bataan, people are also frugal, you are like Ilokanos. So they say. “Why does the President have so much work done?” “It’s because,” said one, “he is Ilokano. so all he does is save and save.”

We really need to save, that money is not your own. That is the people’s money, the money of the citizens. So is this money. This money being used here is not our money. The money that is being spent here is not our money. This money comes from various nations. And therefore must we use it properly. There must never be any question at all about its proper expenditure.

So I told the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees, we don't want to hold that money. Something bad might happen. So I said, he should hold the money and he should spend it. If it gets lost, it's his fault and not ours.

But whatever happens, this money being used here does not come from the coffers of our government. It is a contribution from various countries. All those ambassadors I called, all of them are contributing to our work here. Because this is a project of various countries around the world. So help us show and demonstrate to the whole world that Filipinos can be trusted with anything, with honor and with money. Honor and money can be entrusted to the Filipinos. That's what we need to show.

I thank the Ministry of Human Settlements. You know, at times like these, my spouse remembers me. She already carries my food because I still have a lot of work in my office. So I'm happy to be back in Bataan. You don't know that a great joy fills my heart every time I come to Bataan. Why? Because I am alive, I should have died here with you if I had been unlucky. But with the help of God, I am still here.

Back then, we were just walking around with torn clothes, hungry, almost dying. How many wounds did I have? About five wounds on my body. Now I'm riding a helicopter, imagine that. Isn't that wonderful? I am very happy.

I observed Bataan while on a helicopter and I remarked that it was wonderful to be alive. That is why I am happy to speak to you, because here in Bataan, during the battle at Mount Samat, I was one of those who swore that we would all die here. Five thousand of us swore. Aren't you the soldiers who were with me then? When we were about to surrender, we said, since we will die anyway even if we surrender, let us just kill ourselves here at Mount Samat. All five thousand soldiers swore that they would die here at Mount Samat. No one would be saved. Everyone who wanted to live had already left. We would fight until death. The bad thing was, I was hit severely. I lost consciousness. When I woke up, well, the enemy was facing me and stabbing me with a bayonet. We were made to walk in the Death March and until now, I still don't know why I lived.

I had a companion, who had seventeen bayonet wounds, and he stood up with me. I said, “Let’s walk, don’t show that you’re wounded.” We walked, and we were included in the Death March, carrying him on my back. I carried Priming San Agustin for the length of fifty kilometers.

Now I am here, giving a speech, and you are listening. You are in the heat, while I am in the shade. It is wonderful to remember all the hardships that happened in your life, and then helping those who are also unfortunate and have no country.

Therefore, I thank you, all of you who helped me when I was just a soldier with no duty other than to fight; and to our Almighty God who gave me the opportunity to show everyone that our race knows how to rely on itself for progress and success. That is what we think needs to be shown to the public. And this center is a symbol of the success of our race.
