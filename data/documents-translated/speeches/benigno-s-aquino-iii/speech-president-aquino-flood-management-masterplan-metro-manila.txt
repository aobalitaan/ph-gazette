Speech of the Honorable Benigno S. Aquino III, President of the Philippines, regarding the Flood Management Masterplan for Metro Manila and the inauguration of the new RTU building.

When I was listening to the speech of our Honorable President, he said that it seems part of the job is to insult, belittle, slander, and so on. I said, I'm not alone. [Applause] He was right: It seems easier to insult than to work. Let's see if insults are edible.

You know, I'm just looking at this 11-story building, I clearly remember the groundbreaking, 2010. Because in 2010, the budget left to me was less than ten percent of the National Budget. I entered office, June 30 at noon, 2010. That means, either six or eight percent of the National Budget was left to us, to spend for the entire six months of 2010. Boyet came in, we needed about P600 million here. I said, “Boyet, with that P100 billion, I’m not even sure if the salaries of government employees for these six months have been paid. Let's study it.” But because of Boyet’s diligence, you now have a new building; [looking around] all the equipment; he told me that even the generator that can run the entire building is installed; it’s even air-conditioned, the classroom [applause]–that’s why Speaker [Belmonte] is looking at me angrily. Speaker, the school I came from, Ateneo, probably still doesn’t have air conditioning until now. [Laughter]

I agree that we should perhaps consider the name, “RTU.” Because in the military and police, “RTU” means “return to unit.” [Laughter] Usually, someone has committed a mistake. But perhaps “RizalTech,” like “CalTech” would be good. It seems you agree. Of course, that is your decision.

Before we went here to Mandaluyong, we came from inspecting the in-city housing for our countrymen in Manggahan, Pasig. This housing is part of our extensive strategy—led by the DILG and the NHA—to move those living in danger zones like the Manggahan Floodway away from the risk of flooding, and to build back their communities better.

The place we visited, if you go to the Manggahan Spillway, the residences of these countrymen are not elevated above the Manggahan Spillway. Those being relocated are inside the Manggahan Spillway. So they will be living in the path of the water, right? When the water flow is very strong, isn't it? Truly, inspired by the inspiration given to us by Jesse Robredo, we really found a solution for our countrymen to be placed in proper housing.

You know, earlier our good Secretary Babes Singson mentioned—you know, next year, on July 1, 2016, I expect that when I have a conversation with a 'babes,' it won't be Babes Singson anymore. [Laughter and applause] But I have been hoping for this for a long time, so please don't be too impatient. [Laughter]

Anyway, let's go back to the problem. Earlier we were talking about flooding. According to the DPWH, we do not have sufficient drainage capacity for the entire National Capital Region and neighboring towns. I emphasize: Our canals and esteros cannot handle the large volume of water. That's why, when the rain is heavy, and water flows down from the Sierra Madre, rivers overflow; some parts of Metro Manila are submerged, causing illness and severe traffic. Laguna Lake, which could have absorbed the water, is also silted, so the water level in the lake rises quickly and floods the houses around it. Manila Bay, which is another outlet, also has many low-lying coastal areas around it that are easily flooded, especially if accompanied by high tide. Now, our solution: We need a comprehensive, complete, and clear Flood Management Master Plan that Secretary Babes Singson presented earlier. This includes the ongoing Main Drainage Project for Mandaluyong.

Certainly: Water needs to have a flow and a destination whenever there is a flood. But the reality—and those who have flown over Metro Manila know this—is there still available space to build infrastructure in the National Capital Region? We can build cisterns like the one in Fort Bonifacio there in Burgos Circle, but that comes with digging, which will further narrow the traffic.

Since we are talking about traffic, here is a report from the DTI: Of course, as always, there is good news and bad news. Let's start with the good news: More than 22,400 new vehicles or cars are sold every month. Of course, if you have a new car, you want to use it. I would also add, almost 100,000 new motorcycles every month this year 2014. The total of that is: 1.2 million new motorcycles last year. The result: Of course, it's even more congested on the roads. And naturally, because of the traffic, the vehicle and motorcycle you saved up for so hard will wear out faster because it's just stuck in traffic.

The ideal situation: Add more bridges and widen the streets; but even this, it will take years to implement and there will be a lot of discussion, especially regarding the issue of right of way. Let's take the case of the Guadalupe bridge. The number of vehicles that should pass through it each day is 200,000; but the number that passes through it throughout the day is around 250,000. It really exceeds the capacity of our bridge. That bridge also needs to be retrofitted because it could collapse in an earthquake. We need to build another bridge that will cross the Pasig River, from Global City to Shaw, but there are objections to this because they say they will be affected. Let me also share: I will soon be presented with various plans to alleviate the congestion of vehicles. Mine: The most radical here is to divide the number of vehicles traveling—alternate the use of odd and even license plates on our roads each week. Traffic will definitely ease because half of the vehicles will be gone, but many will surely protest because they won't be able to use their cars.

That is what we are facing. In the fight against floods, up to the laying of infrastructure, and even in regulating traffic, the prevailing mindset is this: “We are okay with the solution, we are okay with the construction, just don't let us get stuck in the implementation.”

How will we do everything if everyone says that the construction of what we need should not be done by us? You will fly back to the National Capital Region, please look at the vacant lots. You will probably see the Rizal Monument. You will probably also see several cemeteries. And those cemeteries are also getting crowded.

So, let me repeat: Regarding flooding, it’s the simplest thing for us to understand—it comes from the Sierra Madre, various rivers, different tributaries, different creeks will pour down. During Ondoy, the volume that flowed was three times more than what the waterways could handle. The water needs a place to go. Now, our drainage here, when we look at it, there are a lot of drainage systems, [that’s good]. There are many buildings, and various other infrastructure with drainage, but the key there is: to dispose of it in the nearest body of water, without anyone calculating the capacity of that body of water. In the entire NCR, only Manila and part of Makati have what is called a sewerage and septic system. Of course, a comprehensive plan needs to be made, and they have already done it, we just need to implement it. There is really logic in putting these systems in place. It’s a long process because the entire National Capital Region and the neighboring provinces still need to be addressed for a complete solution. But all those solutions, including finding funds for it, we can all do; when the time comes, there will be lawsuits left and right to get the right of way, because our cooperation only lasts as long as we are not inconvenienced. It’s difficult to have no one inconvenienced.

I admit: We want to address all problems—and if possible, we should have done this yesterday. But there are limitations to what we can do, and the implementation of a step cannot be done immediately. We are striving to address everything that can be addressed, and my appeal is: Cooperate and increase understanding and patience. With your contribution, we can ensure that we all progress together.

Bosses, on the Straight Path, our goal is: to correct what needs to be corrected; to fix what can be fixed and what should be fixed. Along with this is the investment in a better and more developed future for every Filipino. This is the essence of our inauguration of the Sen. Neptali A. Gonzales Academic Hall of Rizal Technological University today. [Applause] We were the ones who broke ground on this, and you can clearly see the result of our cooperation.

This will serve as the new academic building for those studying education, as well as Information Technology. We are grateful, first of all, to your good Representative Boyet Gonzales, who really focused on this project, and the inauguration is just right before his birthday on Saturday. [Applause] We also thank Speaker Sonny Belmonte for his support, and for prioritizing Mandaluyong before Quezon City [Laughter and applause]. I suppose that means Quezon City needs double the support. [Laughter] The most important thing: This building is the result of the contributions of the people of Mandaluyong. So, thank you very much. [Applause]

It is impressive that we were able to build such a large building, especially since it is crowded in the city. Another impressive thing about this project: We were told that RizalTech's I.T. facilities will be at par with those of other well-known universities. But for me, the greatest success of this facility is that everyone who will benefit from it, because they will share what they learn with the next generation in time.

This new center of education in your city will be visited by experts. Because the equipment is more modern for teaching, they will be able to impart knowledge to students more efficiently. Those who are taught, upon returning to their communities, will be able to share what they have learned. Because of this, the transmission of knowledge to more of our countrymen will be accelerated. This is what we call the multiplier effect, where the benefits received by a few are passed on and inherited by many.

This reminded me of what my father said: “Once you possess knowledge, it is yours forever.” So, to the youth and students who are the primary beneficiaries of this new facility: You now have the tools to maximize your learning. My challenge to you: Use what you have learned to deliver greater benefits and services to your fellow human beings. In your help, expect those you have empowered to stand on a higher platform; and in that way, you can ensure that they reach higher dreams for our country.

As President, my objective remains unchanged: to bequeath a Philippines that is better than the one we inherited; to see the aspirations of the ordinary Filipino within reach, because he is supported by the State and possesses the capacity to fulfill them. The housing this morning, our Flood Management Master Plan, this very building—and even other achievements in the fields of economy and infrastructure, the developing Armed Forces and strengthened PNP, the millions of Filipinos covered by CCT and able to receive free medical treatment thanks to PhilHealth—all of these and many more serve as concrete proof of what we can achieve if we further strengthen our unity along the Straight Path.

The point here is: If the implementation of good plans is disrupted because we place corrupt or unreliable individuals in power, who can say that the needs of the Filipino will truly be met when we step down from office? But if we side with those who are honest and have proven their competence, we will surely reach our aspirations more quickly.

In my most recent SONA, we said: We have come so far, but this is just the beginning. In the remaining time of my term, rest assured that we will double our zeal and further strengthen our commitment to the nation, while simultaneously encouraging our countrymen to remain on the Straight Path in order to expand the benefits it brings. I am confident that: With the guidance of the Lord, and with our unwavering support for good governance, we will surely reach the great potential of our race; we will surely pass on a better Philippines to those who come after us.

In closing, let me revisit the words of our esteemed president. He said that when Boyet asked me about this building, the enrollment at Rizal Technological University was 19,000. Now it is 30,000. That seems to be proof, based on data, that we have also reduced the number of out-of-school youth.

Now, I am also thinking about this. It seems our good President and Boyet Gonzales are also talking. Perhaps next week, they will say: Our population has doubled, so double our building as well. [Laughter and applause] Let me just address Quezon City first. [Laughter] You who have been under umbrellas for a while now, we are all sweating anyway. I don't want to ask if there is no auditorium here, lest it be added to the buildings I need to construct. [Applause]

I would just like to emphasize: In our system, all we need to show is the need, and we will find a way to address it. And since 2010, when this was first mentioned, all I received was debt. And someone asked me for funding. I thought: Where will we get all the funding for this? But you are all witnesses: We were able to do this because we worked together. We were able to address unemployment and so much has really changed in our society. But the best thing I want you to think about: All of this is just the beginning. But it is up to you if we will continue this.

Good day to all. Thank you very much.
