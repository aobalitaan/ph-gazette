Speech of the Honorable Benigno S. Aquino III, President of the Philippines, at the graduation of the Philippine Military Academy Pudang Kalis Class of 2013

[Declared at Fort del Pilar, Baguio City, on March 17, 2013]

I couldn't help but ask our Secretary of National Defense earlier if you were imitating his disposition, because in all the graduations I've attended, you were the only one who graduated looking like you had a “tiger look.” Did you graduate with resentment? I suppose you are just professional.

It is an annual tradition for Presidents to face the cadets here at Fort Del Pilar, to welcome and honor those graduating from the Philippine Military Academy. It is the third time I have been given this honor, and I will not tire of being with you—not only as your Commander-in-Chief, but also to personally witness a success collectively achieved by the Filipino people.

I say this because isn't your march onto the stage earlier a symbol of the contribution of a large society towards achieving your aspirations? It is true that you went through the exams, you woke up early for physical training, and you carried a lot of sacrifices on your shoulders to reach this point; my admiration for what you have been through is undeniable. But also think about this: Before you finished high school and were accepted as plebes here at the PMA, your families also went through sacrifices so you could study, and their support continues to this point. Your teachers and counselors also stayed up late to ensure they had something meaningful to teach you. The entire nation shouldered the construction and operation of the Philippine Military Academy, and will continue to contribute throughout your careers as members of the Armed Forces of the Philippines.

These are just examples of the journeys taken by your valedictorian, Jestoni Lanaja. His upbringing was not luxurious. One of his brothers did not get to attend college, and one of his sisters could not have her disability treated at a hospital. But through gathering tuba, his father was able to support Jestoni until he became a cadet here at PMA; and now, he holds the Presidential Saber, a symbol of the honor and responsibility that comes with being class valedictorian.

Prolen Bonacua is also a success story, for example, who even before being assigned as a soldier, already faced a battle and overcame Hodgkin’s lymphoma. Now, he will graduate as third in your class. I am sure that the honor and joy he feels now is equal to, if not greater than, that of his father, a machinist, and his mother, a factory worker—who both toiled to ensure that Prolen would not face cancer alone, and that opportunities would open for their child, brought about by a good education.

The Pudang Kalis Class of 2013 is not lacking in stories of hardship and triumph: from Maryam Balais, your salutatorian who lost a hand during training, to Cadets Aragona, Gonzales, Landicho, and others of you who were once Jollibee crew members before entering the PMA—all of you are beneficiaries not only of your own efforts, but also of the contributions of your fellowmen. That is why the challenge to you now is: May the accountability and contribution shown by those who helped you give birth to a deep sense of responsibility in your hearts and minds, so that you too will dedicate effort and time to your fellowmen. The nation collectively achieved this success of yours; I hope that the focus of each of your actions will also be collective—because isn't it true that all problems stem from thinking only of self-interest?

Let's just look, for example, at the situation in Sabah. Did those who initiated this think about how many would be affected by causing trouble? There are an estimated hundreds of thousands of Filipinos residing and earning a living peacefully in Sabah; what if our neighbor suddenly sends them home, a neighbor we have been cultivating trust with for a long time? We know how complex this issue is: Would the Prime Minister of Malaysia simply agree to relinquish a place that has been under their laws for so long? Would the President of the Philippines simply let go of the claim to ownership? Put yourselves in my place: whether the grievances of those who went there are legitimate or not, how will this be weighed against the lives and livelihoods that will be put in danger if conflict breaks out? We all know that every action has a reaction, and there are problems that cannot be solved quickly—problems that will only give rise to other problems if force or recklessness is used. What is needed: careful and honest analysis and discussion, so that the right solution can mature.

Did the promoters of this venture consider this while encouraging and fueling it—while detailing the enormous amount of money needed to hire a vessel, buy fuel, provisions, firearms and ammunition? You studied this in your Logistics classes; you know the preparation needed to transport two hundred people across the sea, and to arm some of them. What, then, went through the minds of those behind this incident?

The clear thing is: Whoever masterminded this only thought of their self-interest, while jeopardizing the welfare of their fellow citizens. And even if we believe that those who went there simply wanted to take a stand—wouldn’t it be more productive if that stand was taken through proper dialogue, and if it followed the law and clear policies?

Let us look at the situation in the West Philippine Sea as an example. Isn't it, like in Sabah, that we are promoting a stance based on a rules-based approach, and focused on peaceful resolution, in Bajo de Masinloc? If we resort to thuggery and recklessness, the problem will only escalate, and will likely be passed on to the next generation. We have also seen the positive results of talking to our fellow Filipinos who want to advance the Bangsamoro: Heartfelt dialogue is more effective, more productive, and more beneficial for everyone than pointing guns at each other. Calmness and reason are not equivalent to cowardice—rather, they are the measure of true courage and conviction, because in this way, the welfare of not only those in the present is considered, but especially those who will come in the future.

That is precisely the crossroads you will face as soldiers. Will you give in to anger, or will you face challenges calmly? Will you take shortcuts in your work, or will you think about the impact of your actions and decisions on those who come after you? Will you simply pass on the problems that land before you to the next desk, or will you pour all your strength, all your effort, all your “Heart and Honor of the Soldiers of a United Race” to solve them, and not pass them on to the next generation of Filipinos?

As the next leaders of our uniformed ranks, a great responsibility will be placed on your shoulders. And as someone slightly older than you, perhaps this is the lesson I will leave you with: Whenever you face a crossroads, simply put yourselves in the place of the most aggrieved, the most oppressed, the poorest, those most in need of compassion—and surely, it will become clear what is right and what is wrong. Those on the margins of society, our Bosses, the people, will guide our direction; as long as their welfare is in our minds, we will surely not lose our way.

You know, I will be frank with you, being President of the Philippines is not easy. Some say I need to take a vacation, because they notice the depth of my eye bags and especially how much thinner my hair has become. That’s why I am happy to come to the PMA because I have longer hair than you. There is a part of me that agrees with them—but a larger part says that I only have six years in office, six years to lay the foundations for the changes we have long desired. We are almost halfway through our term, and in the face of the successes we are gradually reaping, I am even more encouraged. So, like your forced marches: No matter how heavy my burden, as long as I can still push my right foot forward, and follow it with my left—especially if I know you are behind me and the entire Filipino nation is ready to contribute to the betterment of our society—we will continue to pursue our dreams.

As I have said to those before you: The training is over, the theory is over, your four-year vacation here at Fort Del Pilar is over; it is now time for the real adventure. Go forth and show that our united nation did not err in choosing you as members of the Pudang Kalis Class of 2013 of the Philippine Military Academy.

Thank you very much, and good day to all of you.
